Planning an event

How to brief a bilingual event host

A useful briefing does more than explain the running order. It gives your host a clear picture of the people, the purpose and the language journey.

Matt Beadle · 5 min read

A bilingual event is more than an English programme with a few German sentences added. People arrive with different levels of fluency, different expectations and different reasons for being there. A good briefing helps the host connect those perspectives.

Start with the audience, before the agenda.

Tell your host who will be in the room, how comfortable they are with each language and which parts of the event matter most to them. A technically fluent audience can still find a long day in a second language demanding.

That picture helps shape the pace, the length of questions and how much context a guest needs before they speak. It also helps identify where a short recap would be genuinely useful.

Give each language a purpose.

Decide what each language is doing in the programme. Is English the shared language, with some German interviews? Are there two distinct audience groups? Are you offering interpretation? The answers affect how the host introduces guests, moves between sessions and includes audience questions.

Word-for-word repetition can make an event feel slow. A concise summary may serve the room better, but that decision should be made with the event team and any interpreters, rather than improvised without context.

Make names and transitions easy to get right.

Share the correct spelling and pronunciation of names, roles and company terminology. Flag which guests will use which language, and agree how questions from the audience will be handled.

A short conversation before the event can also reveal whether a guest would feel more comfortable answering a question in their preferred language, even if the introduction is in the other one.

A useful briefing pack: the event’s purpose, an audience overview, a running order, speaker details, language choices, pronunciation notes and a clear contact for last-minute changes.

Leave room for the people.

The briefing gives the host a foundation. The live room still needs attention. A guest may need more time, a question may deserve a follow-up, or the audience may respond differently than expected.

Clear preparation makes those adjustments easier. You have a shared understanding of what the event is trying to achieve, so flexibility can serve the purpose rather than distract from it.
