Better conversations

What makes a panel worth listening to?

A panel needs more than interesting people and a row of chairs. It needs a question worth exploring and enough structure for the audience to follow.

Matt Beadle · 4 min read

A room can contain a huge amount of expertise and still struggle to produce an engaging panel. The challenge is to turn that expertise into a conversation with direction.

Give the conversation a central question.

A broad subject such as “the future of leadership” is a starting point. A question such as “what should a new leader do differently on Monday?” gives the participants something more specific to work with.

The central question does not have to appear in every exchange. It helps the moderator decide where to go next and what to leave out.

Prepare people, without scripting their answers.

A short preparation conversation can establish each guest’s perspective, the examples they might bring and where their views differ. It also gives them a chance to ask questions about the format.

That is useful preparation for a live exchange. A sequence of rehearsed mini-presentations has a different feel. The moderator needs enough understanding to ask a worthwhile follow-up, while leaving space for an unexpected answer.

Ask for something the audience can picture.

Specific examples give listeners something to engage with. “Can you describe a moment when that became difficult?” often opens a more useful discussion than another invitation to explain a general principle.

The next question should respond to what was actually said. Listening is part of the structure: it helps the panel build a shared conversation rather than take turns delivering unrelated points.

Make disagreement useful.

Different perspectives can create interest when the audience understands what is at stake. A moderator can ask where two approaches diverge, which assumptions sit behind them and when each might be useful.

The aim is to help people understand the issue more clearly. There is no need to manufacture an argument when the real differences are interesting enough.

Before booking a panel, ask: what should the audience understand, question or feel able to do by the end?

Finish with something concrete.

A closing question can help the room turn the discussion into a takeaway: a decision to consider, a question to ask their team or a first step they could try. A clear ending respects the time everyone has invested.
